# The Either-Way Test

**From the episode:** *Pick what stays true either way.*

You cannot find out what happens next, and you still have to decide tonight. This
page takes the decision away from the forecast — one question, applied to whatever
you were about to spend months on.

- **What it is:** one question asked of three or four things you are considering.
- **Who fills it in:** you, alone. Nobody has to agree with your answers.
- **When:** before you commit the next block of evenings, and any time you catch
  yourself reading the argument again instead of doing the thing.
- **Time:** fifteen minutes.

## How to fill it in

1. **Write down what you are actually considering.** Real candidates only — the
   course you have open, the thing your friend suggested, the path you keep coming
   back to. Three or four rows. Not everything you have ever thought about.
2. **For each one, write what it gives you that is a fact about the work.** How
   something behaves under load, why one design costs more than another, what a
   trade-off does. Facts about the work survive whichever way the field goes.
3. **Then write what it gives you that is a fact about a product.** How this tool's
   menus are laid out, which flag does what, this year's interface. **This is not a
   bad column** — you need these and you will keep learning them. It is just the
   part with an expiry date.
4. **Now the question, and answer it honestly for each row:** is this still worth
   having if the answer goes the other way? Yes, no, or *partly*. Do not talk
   yourself into a yes.
5. **Look at the rows where you wrote no.** You are not banning them. You are
   deciding, on purpose, how much of your time goes to something whose value depends
   on a forecast nobody has. Write a number of hours next to each and mean it.
6. **Then stop, and start one of them tonight.** The page is not the point. The
   point is that you stop refreshing the argument.

Replace the example.

**What goes in each column:**

- **What I am considering** — a real thing with a name, not a category. "Networking"
  is a category. "The networking module in the course I already paid for" is a row.
- **About the work** — something that would still be true if every tool in the field
  were replaced. If this column is empty, that is a finding, not a failure.
- **About a product** — something true about a specific thing somebody ships. Almost
  everything has some, and that is fine.
- **Worth having either way** — yes, no, or partly. **"I do not know" is legal**, and
  it usually means you have not looked closely enough at the middle two columns yet.

## The one honest caveat

This is slower than being right. If you correctly guess where the field goes and
commit to it, you arrive sooner than someone who chose what survives either branch.
That is a real cost and this page is not going to pretend otherwise.

What you get instead is a path that does not depend on you being right about
something nobody knows. Given that nobody knows, that is usually the better trade —
but it is a trade, and you should make it with your eyes open.

## Rules that keep it honest

1. **Never fill in the middle columns from a course description.** Read what it
   actually covers. Marketing copy is written to sound like column two and deliver
   column three.
2. **An empty "about the work" column is information.** It does not mean do not do
   it. It means you now know what you are buying, and how long it will last.
3. **Do not put a forecast on this page.** No "assuming this keeps accelerating".
   The moment a prediction appears in a box, the page has become the thing it was
   built to replace.

## What this buys you

A decision you can make tonight without knowing what happens next — and a clear
view of which parts of your plan depend on a forecast nobody has. The refreshing
stops, which is worth more than any of the articles were ever going to be.
