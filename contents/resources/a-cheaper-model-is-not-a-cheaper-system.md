# The Switch Sheet

**From the episode:** *A cheaper model is not a cheaper system.*

Somebody is asking you to approve a switch on the strength of a per-unit price. The unit
is a chunk of text the tool reads or writes — the industry word for that chunk is a
**token** — and the price of one says nothing about how many the system will use.
This page fills in the two numbers that decide it and are not on the slide — what
the system will do differently, and what leaving would cost.

- **What it is:** two questions answered in writing before you approve a change.
- **Who fills it in:** whoever proposed the switch. You read it; you do not write it.
- **When:** before the approval, not after the migration. It takes longer afterwards
  and is worth less.
- **Time:** twenty minutes to fill in. Longer if question two needs someone to go
  and find out, which is itself the finding.

## How to fill it in

1. **Write down what is actually being proposed**, in one sentence, without any
   product marketing in it. "Move the summarising step to a cheaper model" is a
   proposal. "Adopt a next-generation stack" is not.
2. **Answer question one from a real workload, or say you have not.** Run the thing
   you actually run — not a benchmark — and record whether it retries more, chains
   more steps, or re-reads more. **"Not measured yet" is a legal and common answer**
   and it is a perfectly good reason to wait a week before deciding.
3. **Then question two: what would it cost to switch back?** To answer it, somebody
   has to say why the current one was chosen. If nobody can, write **nobody
   recorded this** — that is the most valuable line on the page and it usually
   changes the conversation more than the price does.
4. **Name what still depends on the expensive one.** A behaviour, a format, a
   quality bar somebody once complained about. If the answer is genuinely nothing,
   the switch just got much easier and you have proved it rather than hoped it.
5. **Then decide, and write one sentence saying what you traded.** Approve or wait —
   both are fine. The sentence is what makes this readable in a year.
6. **Then stop.** This is not a migration plan.

## The sheet

**What is being proposed** *(one sentence, no marketing)*:

> ________________________________________

### Question one — what does the system do differently?

**Measured on a real workload?** *(yes / not yet)*

> ________________________________________

**What changed in how much work it does** *(retries, steps, re-reads)*:

> ________________________________________

### Question two — what would it cost to switch back?

**Why was the current one chosen?**

> ________________________________________

**What still depends on that** *(a behaviour, a format, a quality bar)*:

> ________________________________________

**So what would leaving cost us?**

> ________________________________________

### The decision

**We are going to ______________ , and what we traded is:**

> ________________________________________

*(example of an honest answer to question two)*

> *Nobody recorded why. The person who chose it left in the spring. What we know is
> that the support team stopped complaining about the tone of replies the week it
> went in, and nobody knows whether that was this or the prompt change that shipped
> the same day. Cost of leaving: unknown, and we would find out from customers.*

## Rules that keep it honest

1. **"Not measured yet" and "nobody recorded this" are both legal answers**, and
   they are the two that most often change the decision. Never replace either with
   a plausible sentence to make the page look finished.
2. **Question two is not optional.** A switch you cannot reverse priced only on the
   way in is not a saving, it is a commitment.
3. **No product names in the decision line.** Write what you traded, not what you
   bought. Products change; the trade is what you will want to read later.

## What this buys you

You approve or wait on three numbers instead of one — and if the honest answer to
question two is that nobody wrote down why the current thing was chosen, you have
found something worth more than this switch. That gap is what makes every future
migration expensive, and it is fixable in an afternoon once somebody has seen it.
