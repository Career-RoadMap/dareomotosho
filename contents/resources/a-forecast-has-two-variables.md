# The Second Variable

**From the episode:** *Cloud cost forecasting assumes a price that moves.*

Your plan models usage. It also contains a price, and that price is almost always
sitting there as today's rate with nothing written next to it. This page is the
row that names it.

- **What it is:** one block of five lines, added to a capacity plan you already have.
- **Who fills it in:** whoever owns the budget the plan defends — not only the
  person who built the growth curve. Filled in by the modeller it becomes a
  footnote; filled in with the budget holder present it becomes a decision.
- **When:** before the plan is signed off, and again at each review.
- **Time:** an afternoon, most of which is the conversation in line 4.

**There are no figures on this page and that is deliberate.** Any number written
here would be inherited by someone whose estate I have never seen. The blanks are
the work.

## How to fill it in

1. **Line one — the usage assumption, in words.** Not the curve. The sentence
   underneath it. *"We assume usage grows because the new region goes live in the
   second half."*
2. **Line two — the price assumption, in words.** This is the line that does not
   exist in most plans. Write the sentence that is currently implied: *"We assume
   the unit price stays at today's rate."* If your plan does assume a fall, write
   that instead, and write who decided it.
3. **Line three — the arithmetic, not a prediction.** If the unit price moved
   against you by ____%, this plan's number becomes ____. Fill both blanks from
   your own model. **You are not forecasting the move. You are stating the
   exposure.**
4. **Line four — what we would do.** At what point does the answer in line three
   change a decision? Which decision? *"We would delay the second region"* is an
   entry. *"We would monitor it"* is not.
5. **Line five — who watches it.** A name, and how often they look. If nobody
   looks, the row is decoration. **"Nobody yet" is a legal answer** — write it, and
   it becomes the smallest possible action item.

### One filled block, as an example

*This is an example of the SHAPE, not of the numbers — the percentages are left
blank here for the same reason they are blank above.*

| | **Example: a two-region platform plan** |
|---|---|
| 1. Usage assumption, in words | Usage grows through the year because the second region goes live in H2 and traffic is routed to it |
| 2. Price assumption, in words | Unspoken until now. The plan uses today's per-unit rate flat across twelve months. Nobody decided this; it was inherited from last year's template |
| 3. If the price moved against us by ____%, the number becomes ____ | ____ / ____ — filled from our own model, not from a forecast |
| 4. At what point does that change a decision, and which one? | Above the point where the second region stops paying for itself, we would phase it rather than open it in one go |
| 5. Who watches this, and how often | Nobody yet. Proposed: the platform lead, at each quarterly review |

## How to read what you wrote

- **An empty line two** was the whole point of the page. You have found the
  assumption. Write it down and the plan improves before you calculate anything.
- **Line three large and line four empty** means you have measured an exposure
  nobody has decided what to do about. That is worse than not knowing, because now
  it is written down and still unowned.
- **"Nobody yet" in line five** is the most common honest answer and the easiest to
  fix. It costs one name.
- **A small line three is a good result.** Plenty of estates turn out to be robust,
  and you only get to say so because somebody wrote the row.

## The reframe this protects you from

A plan with one variable in it is not a bad forecast. It is not a forecast — it is
an extrapolation, and the difference is not accuracy. A forecast has written down
what it is exposed to.
