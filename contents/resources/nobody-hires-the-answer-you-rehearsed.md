# The One Decision

**From the episode:** *Nobody hires the answer you rehearsed.*

You do not need a better script. You need one real choice you can talk through
when somebody asks. This page builds it — once, tonight, and it works for every
interview after this one.

- **What it is:** four short answers about one decision you have already made.
- **Who fills it in:** you, alone. Nobody has to see this.
- **When:** tonight, not the night before the interview. It needs to settle.
- **Time:** about twenty minutes. Longer if you pick a good one, which is fine.

## How to fill it in

1. **Pick one choice you have already made.** It does not need to be impressive and
   it does not need to be about work. Which certification to start. Which job to
   take. What to learn first. How you fixed something nobody asked you to fix. **It
   only has to be genuinely yours** — the test is whether anyone else in the world
   would have written the same answer.
2. **Write what you chose, in one sentence.** Plain words. No job titles you did not
   have and no dressing it up. This is not the CV.
3. **Write what you gave up to choose it.** Every real choice closed a door — the
   other course, the safer job, the weekend. If you cannot name the door, the
   choice was probably a default rather than a decision, so go back to step 1 and
   pick another.
4. **Write what you would do differently now.** This is the line that changes the
   room, because it is the one almost nobody arrives with. It is not a confession
   and it is not self-criticism. It is evidence that you have thought about it since.
5. **Then say it out loud once, badly, without reading it.** Once. Not twenty
   times. You are checking you can find the thought, not smoothing it — smoothing
   it is the exact thing that emptied the last answer.
6. **Then stop and close the page.** Do not memorise it. If you find yourself
   learning the wording, you have started building a script again.

## The page

**What I chose:**

> ________________________________________

**What I gave up to choose it:**

> ________________________________________

**What I would do differently now:**

> ________________________________________

**The bit I am still not sure about:**

> ________________________________________

*(example of what "genuinely yours" looks like — replace all of it)*

> *I chose to learn the boring administration side before the interesting attack
> side, because the person mentoring me could only meet me on weekends and that was
> what they knew. I gave up about four months of the thing I actually wanted to do.
> I would do it again but I would have asked, out loud, how long it was meant to
> take — I did not know I was allowed to ask that.*

## What to do when you get a follow-up question

You will. That is the point — a follow-up means they got interested, which is what
the whole page is for. Two things hold:

- **"I do not know" is a complete answer**, and it lands better than a guess. Add
  "here is how I would find out" and you have just shown them the thing they were
  actually sampling.
- **You are allowed to think before answering.** A pause reads as consideration.
  Silence is not a scoring event, however loud it feels from the inside.

## Rules that keep it honest

1. **Never invent the decision.** A borrowed story collapses on the second
   question, and the second question is where this page pays.
2. **Do not learn the words.** Know the choice, the cost and the revision. If you
   can recite it, you have rebuilt the problem.
3. **"I am still not sure" is a legal answer** and belongs on the page. Certainty
   you do not have is the easiest thing in the room to see through.

## What this buys you

One thing that is true, that only you could say, that survives a follow-up
question. It works in an interview, and it works in a performance review, a first
conversation with a manager, and the question at the end of a talk — because all of
them are the same room asking the same thing, and almost nobody walks in with an
answer to it.
