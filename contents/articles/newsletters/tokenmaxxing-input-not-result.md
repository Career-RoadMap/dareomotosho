# The receipt on the slide

Somewhere in the next quarterly review, somebody will put up a chart of AI usage and the line will be climbing. Tokens processed, queries run, seats active, money spent. Everybody in the room has been taught to read a line going up and to the right as good news, so for a moment it will feel like progress.

Here is a small experiment worth running before that meeting. Ask the team what the AI budget bought. Write the answer down. Then ask what would be different if the budget had been half the size.

Those two questions sound alike and they are not. The first gets you a receipt, which is a record of what went in. The second is asking for a result, which is what came out. There is now a name going round for treating the first as if it were the second. People call it tokenmaxxing.

## Nobody is hiding anything

It is worth being fair to whoever builds that chart. They are not dressing anything up. They are reporting the one number the tool gives them, and it is an accurate number.

However, we have been here before. When companies first moved onto cloud platforms, the reports counted servers, spend and how busy the machines were. Every figure was real, and every one of them described an input. It took the industry years to notice that none of them said whether the whole thing was worth paying for.

Think of it as a gym membership. The gym will tell you, without being asked, how many times your card went through the turnstile this month. It has no idea whether you got any stronger. The swipe count wins the slide because it is precise, it goes up, and it costs nothing to collect. Finding out whether anything got stronger means going and asking a person. That is slower, it does not automate, and now and then the answer is one nobody in the room wanted.

## One question, not a framework

So what do you ask instead? Something small enough to use in any review: name one decision that went differently because of this.

Not a task that got quicker. A decision.

Three kinds of answer come back. Somebody names one straight away, and you can stop there, because it is working. Somebody has to sit and think about it, which is interesting rather than damning, because value spread across a lot of small moments is real and simply harder to see. Or the answer arrives as a volume, a count of queries or tokens, and then the question has told both of you what you needed to know without anybody having to argue.

Notice what that did not require. You did not need to understand their architecture, which model they chose or how they write their prompts. It moves the conversation away from the people who run the tool and towards the people who use it, and only the second group can tell you whether anything changed.

## It is not a trap

I am not the person to tell you what a decision is worth in your business. That belongs to your finance team, and it is a real discipline.

What I would say is that the question cuts both ways. Sometimes the honest answer is that nothing has changed yet, and you are three months into a twelve-month bet. That is a good answer. The point was never to catch anybody out or to find something to cut. It is how you tell early from wrong, and on a usage chart those two look exactly alike.
