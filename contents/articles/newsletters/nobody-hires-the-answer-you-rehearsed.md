# The week you spent smoothing it

Somebody preparing for interviews right now is doing the same thing tonight that they did last night. Writing the answer out. Saying it to the mirror. Sanding the edges off until it comes out in one piece.

And the strange part is that the smoother it gets, the less it seems to work. Callbacks do not follow. So the obvious conclusion lands: not smooth enough yet. Back to the mirror.

Here is the version of that loop nobody in it can see. An interviewer holding your CV already has your facts. Where you worked, what you studied, in what order. Reading it back to them costs the little time they have with you and tells them nothing the paper had not. What the conversation is actually for is the one thing a page cannot carry: how you think before you have arranged the thinking. Where you pause. What you reach for first. Whether you say you do not know when you do not know.

Now look at what rehearsing does to an answer. It takes out the hesitation. It takes out the sentence you started and then walked back. It takes out the moment where you correct yourself halfway. Rehearsal is, precisely, the removal of everything that is not the finished thought.

Those removed things were the evidence. That is the whole problem in one line. The polish was not hiding your weaknesses; it was deleting the only material the interviewer came to see. So a delivery with no seams in it carries almost no information about you. It reads as a person who has practised, which every other candidate that day has also done, and nothing beyond that.

The repair is not to be more natural, which is advice nobody can act on. It is to prepare a different object. One decision you have actually made. Which course, which job, which tool, how you fixed the thing at work that kept breaking. Small is fine. Then two more moves: name what choosing it cost you, because every real choice closed some door, and say what you would do differently now. Almost nobody arrives with that third part, and it is the part that changes the room.

A decision you can defend invites follow-up questions you have not scripted. That is the price, and it is worth paying, because a follow-up question is interest. It is the thing the polished answer was supposed to produce and never does.
